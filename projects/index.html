When an SEC filing says “third party,” does it actually mean a vendor?

I reviewed cybersecurity incident disclosures directly from SEC EDGAR to separate genuine vendor and contractor failures from filings where “third party” simply means the attacker.

The problem

Keyword matching can create a misleading vendor-risk dataset.

SEC cybersecurity filings frequently use phrases such as “an unauthorized third party gained access.” In that sentence, the third party is the threat actor—not a service provider, contractor, or technology vendor.

This project tests every candidate filing with a simple first question: does the third-party language describe a business relationship, or the attacker? Only then is the event categorized by vendor role, control failure, disclosure detail, and business impact.

Methodology

A defensible, primary-source workflow

The process was designed to be repeatable, auditable, and careful about what the filing actually supports.

  1. 01

    Build the candidate population

    Start with Item 1.05 cybersecurity incident 8-Ks filed after the SEC rule took effect, using public trackers only to locate candidates.

  2. 02

    Read the primary filing

    Open the original SEC EDGAR filing. Do not treat aggregator descriptions as evidence.

  3. 03

    Apply the relevance test

    Determine whether “third party” means a vendor, contractor, subcontractor, or simply the attacker.

  4. 04

    Classify supported details

    Record vendor role, data or operational impact, control-failure category, disclosure detail, and timing only when supported.

  5. 05

    Preserve uncertainty

    Use “unknown” or “insufficient disclosure” instead of forcing a conclusion the filing cannot justify.

  6. 06

    Translate into controls

    Connect observed failures to practical TPRM controls, monitoring questions, and governance decisions.

Preliminary findings

What the first ten filings reveal

01

“Third party” often means attacker

Frontier Communications and OraSure use third-party language for the unauthorized actor. Both would be false positives in a vendor-risk dataset built only through keyword matching.

02

Primary sources change the answer

A secondary tracker attributed ADT’s incident to compromised third-party credentials, but the primary filing does not identify a vendor or credential source.

03

Vendor identity is usually withheld

Many filings disclose only the vendor’s function, platform type, or contractor relationship. Only two cases in the current sample name the vendor directly.

04

Concentration risk becomes financial risk

The CDK Global outage disrupted auto-dealer operations and became an earnings event—showing that third-party cyber risk is not limited to data exposure.

05

Fourth-party risk is hard to see

The FHLB New York filing explicitly describes a “vendor of a Bank vendor,” offering a rare public example of risk propagating beyond a direct supplier.

From disclosure to action

What a TPRM program should do differently

Access

Scope and monitor vendor access

Apply least privilege, review API integrations, monitor contractor activity, rotate credentials, and define rapid offboarding requirements.

Resilience

Measure concentration and substitutability

Identify critical single-vendor dependencies, define manual workarounds, test recovery assumptions, and understand downstream revenue exposure.

Visibility

Extend oversight beyond direct vendors

Require disclosure of critical subcontractors, flow down security terms, and establish notification obligations for fourth-party events.

Governance

Validate evidence before categorization

Separate facts from external reporting, preserve unknowns, document analyst judgments, and keep a traceable source for every row.
