The Crime Shows That Accidentally Led Me to Cybersecurity

Growing up, I watched a ridiculous amount of crime shows. I still do.

Not because I wanted to become a detective, but because I was fascinated by the process. How did they know where someone had been? How did they recover deleted messages? How did a single piece of digital evidence completely change the direction of an investigation?

Back then, I had no idea that digital forensics investigator was an actual career. I didn’t even know what cybersecurity was. If someone had asked me, I probably would have said something like “hacking.” A bunch of people sitting behind computers, breaking into systems.

That was the extent of my understanding.

A few years later, that same curiosity is what led me to major in the field.

The more I learned, the more I discovered that cybersecurity wasn’t just about protecting computers. It was about understanding people, technology, risk, and information. It was about finding answers hidden in data and helping organizations make better decisions before problems became disasters.

One of the biggest misconceptions is that every cybersecurity role involves hacking. In reality it’s an entire ecosystem. Some professionals investigate cybercrime and analyze digital evidence. Others monitor networks, manage risk, handle compliance, or help organizations prepare for audits and regulations. The paths are more varied than most people realize.

As I moved through college, I found myself drawn to both the technical and analytical sides of the field. Security isn’t just about stopping attacks. It’s about understanding risk, identifying weaknesses, communicating findings, and helping organizations improve before something goes wrong.

At its core, cybersecurity is about solving problems.

Whether you’re investigating an incident, assessing risk, or helping users stay safe, you’re always trying to answer the same question: What happened, what could happen next, and what can we do about it?

If you’re considering this field, you don’t need to fit a stereotype. You don’t need to be a coding genius or have your entire path figured out.

Sometimes all it takes is curiosity.

For me, it started with a crime show and one simple question:

“How did they figure that out?”
